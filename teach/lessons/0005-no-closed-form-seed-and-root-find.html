Lesson 5 · From Equations to Algorithm

No closed form: seed and root-find

Lesson 2 said two runs give two equations for two unknowns — "solvable." Today's the catch: solvable in principle isn't the same as solvable with algebra. This is where the engine stops being a formula and starts being a small piece of numerical software.

Why you can't just solve it by hand

Write out what each run gives you, from the boxed equation in Lesson 2:

$$e^{\,b_{e,i}\,t'_{R,i}/t_0} - 1 = b_{e,i}A, \qquad A \equiv k_0-\frac{\tau}{t_0}, \qquad b_{e,2}=b_{e,1}/\beta$$

Two equations, two unknowns ($b_{e,1}$ and $A$) — but $b_{e,1}$ sits inside two exponentials running at different, incommensurate rates ($t'_{R,1}/t_0$ vs. $t'_{R,2}/(\beta t_0)$). Try to eliminate $A$ and you're left with one equation that mixes those two exponentials with no algebraic move that isolates $b_{e,1}$. That's what "transcendental" means here in practice: not unsolvable, just not solvable by rearranging symbols. It needs a number, found numerically.

The seed: the same intuition from Lesson 2, made literal

Recall the "two points define a line" picture from Lesson 2. If the solute is well retained ($b_e A \gg 1$), the "+1" in the retention equation is negligible, and the algebra does go through cleanly:

$$S_e \approx \frac{\ln\beta}{\varphi_{e,1}-\varphi_{e,2}}$$

That's not a new idea — it's the two-points-define-a-line picture written out as an actual formula: $S_e$ is (a constant) divided by the separation between the two sampled points. This closed form is real and useful, but only as a starting guess, because "+1 is negligible" is an approximation that degrades badly for smaller $k_0$:

Guillarme et al. (2022) give the threshold explicitly: the approximation becomes tolerable once log₁₀k₀ exceeds about 2.1 (k₀ ≈ 126) — well suited to proteins, marginal for small molecules, which is exactly your regime.

Where your own peaks sit Your three fitted peaks have log₁₀k₀ ≈ 2.76, 3.24, and 4.76 — all above Guillarme's 2.1 threshold, so the seed alone would probably already be decent for this particular sample. That's exactly the trap: a general-purpose engine can't assume every future sample will be this well-behaved. Someone will eventually enter an early-eluting peak with k₀ = 8, where the seed alone is off by 138%. SPEC.md §3 states the resulting policy plainly: "The closed form alone is forbidden as a final answer."

The exact solve

The seed feeds a proper root-find. Define, for $b>0$:

$$g(b) = e^{\,b\,t'_{R,1}/t_0} - 1 - \beta\Big(e^{\,b\,t'_{R,2}/(\beta t_0)}-1\Big)$$

$g(b)=0$ is the exact two-run condition, just rearranged so both runs are on one side. Three facts about its shape turn into guardrails in the code, not just curiosities:

  • $g(0)=0$ always — a mathematical artifact, not a real fit. The search must exclude it and bracket strictly above zero.
  • $g$ dips negative right after 0, because the shallower gradient always elutes a peak later than the steeper one — a physically necessary fact, not a coincidence.
  • A positive root exists (and is unique) only if $\varphi_{e,1} > \varphi_{e,2}$ — the steeper gradient must elute the peak at the higher %B. If that fails, the data are inconsistent with LSS entirely — usually because two peaks got swapped between the two runs during tracking. That's not a numerical failure to paper over; it's a diagnostic the engine should surface as "check peak tracking," not silently patch with a least-squares fudge.

The seed from the previous section gives a good starting bracket; Brent's method — a standard root-finder that never diverges the way Newton's method sometimes can, and costs little extra since $g$ is cheap to evaluate — finds the exact root inside it. Once you have $b_{e,1}$, recovering $k_0$ and $S_e$ is exact, no approximation left:

$$A=\frac{e^{\,b_{e,1}t'_{R,1}/t_0}-1}{b_{e,1}}, \qquad k_0=A+\frac{\tau}{t_0}, \qquad S_e=\frac{b_{e,1}t_{G,1}}{t_0\Delta\varphi}$$

The self-check that catches your own bugs

After solving, the research doc's recommended discipline is to plug the fitted $(k_0,S_e)$ back into the original retention equation for both runs and confirm you recover the two input $t_R$ values to within 1e-8. This "round-trip" check is a generalizable idea worth keeping regardless of domain: it doesn't validate the model against reality, it validates that the code that just ran actually solved the equation it was supposed to, catching a silent implementation bug before it becomes a silently wrong retention-time prediction.
