Lesson 2 · The Science Model

Why two runs are enough

The engine preview from Lesson 1 fit each peak from exactly two gradient runs. Here's the model that makes that possible, and why it needs exactly two — not one, not five.

The thing you already know

Run the same compound isocratically at a few different %B and you already know what happens: more organic, faster elution; less organic, slower. If you plotted log k against %B for one compound across several isocratic runs, you'd get something close to a straight line over a moderate range. That empirical observation — not a first-principles law, just a very good fit over the ranges chromatographers actually work in1 — is the entire content of the linear solvent strength (LSS) model.

1. Traced to Snyder, Dolan & Gant, J. Chromatogr. A 165 (1979) 3–30. It's a workhorse approximation, not exact — Lesson 7 in this workspace covers where it breaks down.

The model, in the convention the engine uses

$$\ln k = \ln k_0 - S_e(\varphi - \varphi_0)$$

$\varphi$ is just %B ÷ 100. This is a straight line in $(\varphi, \ln k)$ space, with two parameters per compound:

  • $k_0$ — the retention factor at the gradient's starting composition $\varphi_0$. Anchored inside the range you actually ran, which is why the engine fits this instead of the more traditional $k_w$ (retention extrapolated to pure water) — $k_w$ is a further extrapolation with much larger uncertainty.2
  • $S_e$ — the slope: how fast $\ln k$ falls as $\varphi$ rises. Chemically, this is that compound's sensitivity to organic content. Small, non-polar-ish differences between your three unknowns show up here.
2. Poole & Atapattu (2022): "log $k_w$ cannot be recommended as a descriptor of solute properties since it has no clear connection to a real distribution system." $S_e$ converts to the base-10 $S$ you'd normally quote via $S = S_e/2.303$.
A straight line needs two points A line has two unknowns — an intercept and a slope. One measurement can't pin down both; you need two. That's the whole reason the method needs two runs, restated as geometry instead of algebra.

What one gradient run actually measures

A gradient run isn't an isocratic measurement at one $\varphi$ — the composition changes continuously while the band migrates. But each run still produces exactly one number for a given peak: its retention time $t_R$. Converting that $t_R$ into where on the $(\varphi, \ln k)$ line it sits requires knowing $t_0$ and $\tau$ (the pre-gradient period — dwell plus any hold; more on that in Lesson 3) and the gradient's steepness. The full relationship — sourced, not derived here — is:

$$t_R = \tau + t_0 + \frac{t_0}{b_e}\ln\!\Big[b_e\big(k_0-\tfrac{\tau}{t_0}\big)+1\Big], \qquad b_e = \frac{t_0\,\Delta\varphi\,S_e}{t_G}$$

Don't memorize this yet — the point right now is just what's on each side. One equation, one measured $t_R$, two unknowns ($k_0$, $S_e$) buried inside $b_e$. Exactly as underdetermined as the line-with-one-point picture above.

Why the second run has to be steeper, not just different

Look at $b_e$: it depends on $S_e$ and on $t_G$. Two runs that differ only in $t_G$ — same column, same $\varphi_0/\varphi_f$, same everything else — give two different $b_e$ values for the same peak, which means the band elutes at two different points along its own $(\varphi,\ln k)$ line. Two equations, two unknowns, solvable — and because $k_0$ and $S_e$ genuinely differ per compound, this works independently for every peak in the mixture, using the same pair of runs.
